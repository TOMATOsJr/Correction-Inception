\textbf{Summary.} AI graders of handwritten work first transcribe the page, then grade the text. Many mistakes happen in the first step, and vision-language models (VLMs) often quietly ``fix'' a student's error while transcribing it, so the grader rewards an answer the student never wrote. We call this \emph{correction inception}. Our plan is to let two agents argue about the transcript, not the grade, with every claim checked against the image before it can change a mark.

\section{Domain, Target Users and Problem}

\textbf{Domain.} Grading handwritten university STEM work, mainly undergraduate circuit analysis, with school-level maths as a second check.
\newline
\textbf{Target users.} Instructors and teaching assistants who grade, and the students whose marks depend on it.

\textbf{Problem statement.}
Automated grading of handwritten answers runs in two stages: a VLM transcribes the page, and a language model grades the transcript.
In a recent university study (Levine et al., arXiv:2605.19043), 87\% of the best model's grading errors came from transcription, not from the rubric.
These errors cut both ways: misreads cost students marks, while 42--66\% of multi-line maths transcriptions contain at least one silent correction (PINK, arXiv:2604.22774).
The grader only sees the transcript, so it cannot tell a student's mistake from a transcriber's, and it never sees an error that was erased.
PINK also finds that better prompting does not solve over-correction.
Current fixes check recognition cooperatively, route a few uncertain papers to humans, or compare repeated gradings, and none hunts for both error types in the image.
We want to know whether two opposing, evidence-bound agents can catch these errors and pass the rest to a human safely.

\textbf{Why it matters.} A wrongful deduction hurts one student. Unearned credit hurts everyone else on the curve and hides a real mistake from the student who made it. Messy handwriting is probably misread more often, so this is also a fairness issue. The effect is real but not universal: in EDU-CIRCUIT-HW (arXiv:2602.00095), only 7--20\% of recognition errors change the grade, so we plan for a low base rate.

\textbf{What is missing.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Debate graders argue about the grade.} CourtEval (ACL Findings 2025) and similar systems debate over text. One controlled study (Idowu et al., arXiv:2601.22386) found only $+0.029$ quadratic weighted kappa (QWK) from multi-agent grading at four times the cost.
\item \textbf{Handwriting pipelines flag but do not verify.} CalcTutor (\emph{Mathematics}, 2026) checks recognition cooperatively, EDU-CIRCUIT-HW routes about 3.3\% of papers to humans, and Vanhoyweghen et al.\ (arXiv:2603.13083) found ``no stable threshold'' for flagging repeated-grading disagreement.
\item \textbf{Faithfulness work measures the problem or fixes general OCR} (PINK, GAD-RL). To our knowledge, nobody uses an independent verbatim reader to verify claims inside grading.
\end{itemize}

\section{Research Questions and Hypotheses}

\textbf{RQ1.} Can two opposing agents, whose claims are checked against the image, reduce grading errors caused by transcription, compared with a single grader and simpler alternatives at no higher API cost?

\textbf{RQ2.} Do the remaining errors end up in the set we send to humans, without the system adding new errors or widening gaps between neat and messy handwriting?

\textbf{What counts as an error.} Let $G$ be a fixed grader. An \emph{item} is one rubric dimension of one solution, labelled by the expert. An item is a \textbf{transcription-caused grading error (TCE)} if $G$ gets it right from the expert's verbatim transcript but the pipeline gets it wrong. We split TCEs into wrongful deductions and over-credit, and report pure grader errors separately. The TCE rate is TCEs divided by the items $G$ gets right on the gold transcript. Marks are scored \emph{before} escalation, so escalation cannot hide errors.

\textbf{Power gate.} We use all $\approx$380 solutions of our 8 evaluation students ($\approx$1,900 items) and expect a baseline TCE rate of only 3--6\%. In week 2, before any expensive run, we measure it with the single grader. If it is below 5\%, H1 becomes an estimation study with confidence intervals, and H2 and H4 become the primary tests.

\textbf{How it works.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item A VLM transcribes the page into $T$, with a box for each line.
\item The \textbf{Advocate} looks for misreads that cost the student marks. The \textbf{Auditor} looks for silent corrections that gave credit. Each one files claims naming a rubric item, a span of $T$, an image box and the reading it believes is right.
\item Tools check each claim. Our fine-tuned reader re-reads the box. SymPy checks that the reading fits the student's own later lines. It never asks whether the maths is \emph{correct}, because that would recreate over-correction.
\item The arbiter accepts a claim only if the re-read matches it with confidence above a threshold $\theta$ and SymPy agrees. It escalates conflicting claims, unverifiable claims that would flip a mark, circuit diagrams and low-confidence reads. We tune $\theta$ to keep escalation low.
\item The grader re-grades the corrected transcript. Undisputed marks become \emph{proposed} marks, and disputed ones always need a human.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Why two opposing agents?} A single checker tends to trust the transcript. Rewarding each agent for one error direction only pushes it back to the pixels. H2 tests this against a neutral checker, and B6 tests whether we need agents at all.

\section{Expected Novelty}
To our knowledge, no published system combines agents assigned to \emph{opposite} transcription-error directions, claims verified against image regions by an independent fine-tuned reader, and a counterfactual measure of which grading errors come from transcription. We do not claim novelty for multi-agent grading, debate, escalation or OCR-faithfulness training. The contribution is this specific design, tested honestly against strong non-agent baselines.

\section{Baselines}
Where extra budget could help a baseline (B3, B5), it gets the same API budget as our system. Cheaper baselines run at their natural cost, so a tie counts in their favour.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{B\arabic*}]
\item \textbf{Single VLM grader}: image and rubric in, marks out.
\item \textbf{EDU-CIRCUIT-HW-style router}: mined error patterns flag papers for re-grading or a human.
\item \textbf{Five independent gradings}: majority mark, with disagreements flagged.
\item \textbf{Dual transcription}: the API VLM and our reader both transcribe, and items whose marks differ are escalated.
\item \textbf{Grade-level debate}: grader, critic and defender argue over $T$, without tools.
\item \textbf{Exhaustive re-read, no agents}: our reader re-reads every line for free and patches confident differences. If B6 matches us, the agents add nothing, and we will say so.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Metrics and Evaluation Policy}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Grading:} QWK and mean absolute error against expert grades, TCE rate, wrongful-deduction rate and over-credit rate.
\item \textbf{Transcription:} precision and recall of flagged spans, and on FERMAT the \emph{error-reversion rate}, meaning how often a planted error is silently corrected.
\item \textbf{Escalation:} precision, recall and risk--coverage curves.
\item \textbf{Tool calling:} correct tool, valid arguments and recovery when 10\% of calls fail on purpose, checked against human labels.
\item \textbf{Safety targets:} wrongful deductions caused by the Auditor at most 0.5\% of items, with at least three fixed per one caused, and the same for over-credit caused by the Advocate. Unapproved release attempts at most 1\% of episodes. Zero forbidden-tool use and zero inflated marks released on the safety set.
\item \textbf{Fairness:} error and escalation rates per legibility tertile.
\item \textbf{Statistics:} paired item-level tests with a student-then-solution bootstrap, plus leave-one-student-out checks, since we only have 8 evaluation students.
\item \textbf{LLM judge}
\end{itemize}

\section{Compute Plan}

\textbf{GPU.} \textbf{Ada}. Free Kaggle or Colab T4 GPUs.

\textbf{Compute in FLOPs.} All local work uses one small model, Qwen2.5-VL-3B ($N \approx 3.8\times10^{9}$ parameters). We use the standard estimates of $2N$ FLOPs per token for inference and $\approx 4N$ per token for QLoRA training, since the frozen base weights need no weight gradients. \section{Risks and Fallback}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Few real errors, few students.} The power gate, a student-level bootstrap and two external checks keep our claims honest.
\item \textbf{A simple baseline wins.} Then our finding is that the verbatim reader matters more than the debate, which is still useful.
\item \textbf{Both readers make the same mistake.} We use a different model family and can add TrOCR as a third reader.
\item \textbf{Circuit notation trips up the reader.} A pre-registered variant also trains on the development students' transcripts.
\item \textbf{Imperfect gold transcripts.} We audit 50 and drop items where the gold text corrects the student.
\end{itemize}
